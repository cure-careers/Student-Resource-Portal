Chapter 1

I. Sound vs. Ultrasound

Elastic – An object that returns to its original shape after it is deformed.

Compression – Squeeze air molecules together.

Rarefaction – Stretch air molecules apart.

Sound Wave – A series of compressions and rarefactions.

A sound wave’s behavior and adherence to physical laws and principles are the same regardless of whether or not the wave is an audible sound wave or an inaudible sound wave.

Wave – A rhythmical variation throughout time that carries energy from one place to another.

Mechanical Wave – A wave that requires a medium in which to travel and therefore it cannot propagate in a vacuum.

The molecules in the medium vibrate, striking their neighbors, which in turn vibrate.

Vacuum – Space that has all matter removed from it.

Sound cannot travel through a vacuum. It requires a medium in order to propagate.

Longitudinal Wave – The particles of the medium move back and forth parallel to the direction the sound wave travels.

The molecules in the medium vibrate back and forth in the same direction that the wave is propagating.

Transverse Wave – The particles of the medium move in a direction perpendicular to that of the wave.

If a transverse wave is traveling vertically, then the particles in the wave are traveling horizontally.

Ultrasound – A mechanical, longitudinal wave that requires a medium and normally travels in a straight path unless reflection or refraction changes its direction. It is the rhythmical cycling of acoustic variables which are density, pressure, temperature, and distance or particle motion. It can be continuous or pulsed. Above the range of human hearing: 20,000 Hz (cycles/sec) or greater.

20,000 Hz can also be described as 20 kHz or 0.02 MHz.

Infrasound – Below the range of human hearing. Less than 20 Hz.

Audible Sound – In the range of human hearing.

Between 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz.

II. Acoustic Variables

Acoustic – Related to sound.

Variable – A quantity that can change.

Acoustic Variable – A measurable quantity that changes as a sound wave passes through a medium: pressure, density, temperature, and particle motion.

Pressure – Force applied per unit area.

Units: Pascals (Pa) or lb/sq. in.

Pressure can also be reported in atmospheres (atm) and millimeters of mercury (mmHg).

Units: °C, °F, Kelvin

Heat flows from areas of higher temperature to lower temperature.

Density – Mass per unit volume.

Units: kg/m³ (or g/cm³)

Density = Mass ÷ Volume

or written mathematically:

The highest density is located where a volume contains the largest number of molecules.

Particle Motion – The back-and-forth movement of particles in the medium as sound travels.

Units: cm, ft, miles

III. Pulsed and Continuous Waves

Pulse – A collection of cycles with a beginning and an end that travel together.

Continuous Mode / Continuous Wave – The vibratory motions are produced by the source in an uninterrupted, continuous stream and cannot create an image.

Pulsed Mode / Pulsed Wave – The vibratory motions are produced by the source in short bursts, or pulses, of acoustic energy that are produced and then received by the transducer to construct an image.

There is no difference in the speed of sound (propagation speed) between continuous and pulsed waves.
The period of a wave is a characteristic of each individual cycle in the acoustic signal. Therefore, there is no difference between the period of a continuous or a pulsed wave.
The wavelength is a characteristic of each individual cycle in the acoustic signal. Therefore, there is no difference between a continuous or pulsed wave.
The frequency is a characteristic of each individual cycle in the acoustic signal. Therefore, there is no difference between a continuous or pulsed wave.

Pulsed and Continuous Waves Parameters That Are the Same:

Propagation Speed
Period
Wavelength
Frequency
All are features of a single cycle and are not affected by whether the wave is continuous or pulsed.

V. Parameters of Sound Waves / Propagation of Sound

Hertz – Cycle per second.

Cycle – A complete positive and negative change in an acoustic variable composed of one compression and one rarefaction.

Frequency – The number of complete cycles that an acoustic variable undergoes in one second of time. In other words, the number of events that occur over a duration of time.

Units: Hertz (Hz), Megahertz (MHz), cycles/sec, per second.

Frequency = The reciprocal of period.
If the frequency doesn’t change, then the period doesn’t change and vice versa.

Period – The time required to complete one cycle. Period is equal to one divided by the frequency.

Units: seconds (s), microseconds (μs), any unit of time.

Period = The reciprocal of frequency.
A wave with a frequency of 1 MHz has a period of 1 μsec. A wave with a frequency of 10 MHz has a period of 0.1 μsec.

Wavelength – The length or distance that a sound wave travels in one cycle.

Units: m, mm, any unit of length.

Wavelength can also be reported in miles or meters, although impractical.

Propagate – To travel.

Propagation Speed – The speed at which a sound wave travels through a medium. It is determined by the density and stiffness of the medium.

Units: meters/second (m/s) or any unit of distance over time.

(Speed = Distance / Time)
Propagation Speed (m/sec) = Frequency (Hz) × Wavelength (m)
If propagation speed increases, wavelength increases.

Pulse Duration – The time from the start of a pulse to the end of that pulse. In other words, the span of time required for the transducer to create a pulse.

Units: seconds (s), microseconds (µs), any unit of time.

Pulse Duration does not include the listening time.
Pulse Duration = Pulse Repetition Period – Listening Time
Pulse Duration = Period × # of Cycles in the Pulse

Spatial Pulse Length – The distance from the start of one pulse to the end of that pulse.

Units: Meter (m), Millimeter (mm), any unit of distance.

SPL is directly proportional to the wavelength.
Spatial Pulse Length = # of Cycles in a Pulse × Wavelength

Pulse Repetition Frequency (PRF) – The number of pulses that the transducer creates in one second.

Units: Hertz (Hz), usually kilohertz (kHz), pulses per second, or 1/second.

PRF = The Reciprocal of Pulse Repetition Period.

Pulse Repetition Period (PRP) – The time from the start of one pulse to the start of the next pulse, which includes the time the pulse is on and the time the pulse is listening.

Units: seconds (s), milliseconds (ms), any unit of time.

PRP = The Reciprocal of Pulse Repetition Frequency.

Duty Factor – The fraction of time or percentage of time that the transducer generates a pulse. Duty factor has a value between 0 and 1 (or 0%–100%).

Remember, the only time duty factor is 1 or 100% is with continuous wave ultrasound.
PRP, PRF, and Duty Factor can all be altered by the sonographer when depth changes.
Imaging depth is the main control that sets the maximum PRF: greater depth increases PRP and decreases the maximum PRF. In Doppler, the sonographer can also change the displayed PRF with the scale control, within the limit imposed by depth.
Duty Factor = Pulse Duration / PRP

where:

Pulse Duration (or Pulse Width) = how long the pulse is “on”
PRP = Pulse Repetition Period = total time from the start of one pulse to the start of the next

Meaning in plain terms

The duty factor tells you what fraction of the time the signal is ON.

Example

Pulse Duration = 2 ms
PRP = 10 ms

So the signal is on 20% of the time and off 80% of the time.

Related (common confusion)

Sometimes you’ll also see:

PRF (Pulse Repetition Frequency) = 1 / PRP
Then duty factor can also be written as:

Duty Factor=Pulse Duration×PRF

VII. Intensities

Spatial – Referring to space or distance.

Temporal – Referring to time.

Peak – The maximum value.

Average – The mean value.

Spatial Peak – The intensity located at the center of the beam.

Spatial Average – The mean intensity averaged throughout the beam.

Temporal Peak – The maximum intensity in the pulse, measured at any instant when the pulse is on.

With continuous wave, the temporal average and the temporal peak are the same because the pulse is always “on.”

Temporal Average – The average throughout all time (both transmitting and receiving).

With continuous wave, the temporal average and the temporal peak are the same because the pulse is always “on.”

Pulse Average – The intensity averaged over the duration of a single pulse (only transmitting time).

Intensity Types

SPTP – Spatial Peak Temporal Peak

The largest intensity recorded at any position in space and at any instant in time.

SATP – Spatial Average Temporal Peak

SPTA – Spatial Peak Temporal Average

Affected by both listening and pulsing time; the lowest intensity.

SATA – Spatial Average Temporal Average

SPPA – Spatial Peak Pulse Average

Has a value between SPTP and SPTA. SPPA always exceeds SPTA.
It is only relevant for pulsed ultrasound and meaningless for continuous wave ultrasound.

SAPA – Spatial Average Pulse Average

Ignores receiving time.

Im – The maximum intensity observed in an ultrasonic wave when averaged over the largest half-layer.

Units: All intensities have units of Watts/cm².

Beam Uniformity Coefficient (B.U.C.)

A unitless ratio of the peak intensity divided by the average intensity in space that describes the distribution of an ultrasound beam in space.

It is equal to 1 or greater than 1.
Used to convert Spatial Peak Intensities to Spatial Average Intensities.
B.U.C. = Spatial Peak Intensity ÷ Spatial Average Intensity

Duty Factor

A unitless number between 0 and 1 representing the percentage of time the sound pulse is “on.”

The value of duty factor in a continuous wave can be referred to as 1 or 100% because the pulse is always “on.”
Used to convert Pulse Average Intensities to Temporal Average Intensities.

3 Step Process to Intensity Conversions

Converting PA to TA Intensities

You will be given the SPPA intensity and the Duty Factor.
Multiply the SPPA by the Duty Factor to find the SPTA.
SPPA × Duty Factor = SPTASPPA × Duty Factor = SPTA
When converting PA to TA, use the Duty Factor to solve the equation.

This gives you the SPTA.

Converting SP to SA Intensities

You will be given the SP/SA factor in the question.
Divide the SPTA intensity by the SP/SA factor.
SPTA ÷ SP/SA Factor = SATASPTA ÷ SP/SA Factor = SATA
When converting SP to SA, use the SP/SA Factor.
This gives you the SATA.

IX. Transducers and Transducer Material

Piezoelectric Effect

The ability of piezoelectric crystals to create a voltage when they are mechanically deformed.

Active Element

The piezoelectric crystal itself. Lead Zirconate Titanate (PZT) is the most commonly used material in ultrasound transducers.

Speed of Sound in PZT: approximately 4–6 mm/µs (about 3–4 times faster than in soft tissue).

For pulsed ultrasound, the thickness of the crystal and the speed of the crystal determine the frequency of sound.
For continuous wave ultrasound, frequency is determined by the frequency of the electrical oscillator, or driving signal, applied to the crystal—not by the voltage amplitude.

In other words, the frequency of the acoustic signal produced by the crystal equals the frequency of the electric signal driving the crystal.

Lead Zirconate Titanate (PZT)

A man-made piezoelectric ceramic commonly used in ultrasound transducers.

PZT has a greater impedance than the matching layer, gel, and skin.

Crystal Thickness

Thicker crystals have lower frequencies.
Thinner crystals have higher frequencies.
The frequency of the acoustic wave is partly determined by thickness — different thicknesses produce pulses of different frequencies.

Crystal Speed

Faster crystals Higher frequencies
Slower crystals Lower frequencies
The frequency of the acoustic wave is partly determined by speed — various speeds produce pulses of different frequencies.

Curie Temperature

The temperature above which a piezoelectric crystal loses its polarization and piezoelectric properties.

Approx. 600–700°F or 300–400°C.

Depolarized

Piezoelectric materials lose all piezoelectric properties when heated above the Curie temperature.

Transducer

Any device that converts one form of energy into another.

Damping Element

An epoxy resin combined with tungsten attached to the back of the PZT crystal inside the transducer. It acts to dampen ringing of the crystal, shortening the pulse and improving image resolution.

Damping material increases bandwidth. The wider the bandwidth, the shorter the pulse and the better the image.
The wider the bandwidth, the lower the Q-Factor.
Damping decreases pulse duration and spatial pulse length.

Wire

Provides electrical contact between the ultrasound system and the crystal during transmission and reception.

Case / Insulator

A plastic or metal housing that surrounds and protects the transducer’s internal components, shielding the patient from electric shock and preventing component damage.

Matching Layer

A layer positioned between the crystal and the skin to reduce impedance mismatch and improve sound transmission.

The matching layer is typically one-quarter wavelength thick at the transducer’s center frequency. Its acoustic impedance is between that of the PZT and the patient, which reduces reflection at the transducer surface and improves transmission into the body.

Decreasing order of impedances:

Active Element (PZT) Matching Layer Gel Skin

Bandwidth

The difference between the highest and lowest frequencies within a pulse; it represents a range of frequencies.

Units: Hertz (Hz)

The narrower the bandwidth, the more exact a frequency emitted by a transducer.
Imaging transducers have wider bandwidths (shorter pulses) than therapeutic transducers, which have narrow bandwidths.

Formula:

Bandwidth = Highest Frequency − Lowest Frequency

Q Factor (Quality Factor)

A unitless number representing a transducer’s ability to emit a single (pure) frequency of sound.

Imaging transducers = low Q factor (wide bandwidth)
Therapeutic transducers = high Q factor (narrow bandwidth)
Q Factor = Center Frequency ÷ Bandwidth

Resonant Frequency

The center frequency at which the piezoelectric crystal naturally vibrates most efficiently. For a crystal operating in thickness mode, resonant frequency is determined primarily by crystal thickness and propagation speed within the crystal.

X. Sound Beams

Focus / Focal Point

The most narrow point of the sound beam where the diameter of the beam is smallest.

The location where optimum transverse resolution is obtained.
When a beam undergoes focusing, it narrows in the region close to the end of the near zone and at the beginning of the far zone.

Focal Length

The distance from the transducer to the focus.

Determined by the diameter of the PZT crystal and the emitted frequency.
Crystal Diameter is not the same as Crystal Thickness.
Changes in PRP do not affect the depth of focus. The frequency and diameter of the crystal do.

Crystal Diameter

Larger Crystal Diameter Longer Focal Length
Larger Crystal Diameter Deeper Beam Focus
Smaller Crystal Diameter Shallower Beam Focus

Emitted Frequency

Higher Emitted Frequency Longer Focal Length
Higher Emitted Frequency Deeper Beam Focus
Lower Emitted Frequency Shallower Beam Focus

Near Zone

The portion of the sound beam between the transducer and the focus.

In an unfocused disc transducer operating in continuous mode, at the end of the near zone the beam diameter is one half the transducer diameter. At two near-zone lengths, the beam diameter equals the transducer diameter.

Far Zone

The portion of the sound beam past the near field, in which the diameter of the beam increases as the distance from the transducer increases.

Focal Zone

The narrower portion of the beam that surrounds the focus. This is the region of best image resolution.

Diffraction

The pattern of divergence or spreading of a sound wave as it propagates, produced by a small sound source.

Huygen’s Principle

The principle that describes why imaging transducers do not diffract significantly.

Each PZT particle of the single crystal produces its own wave. These numerous waves interfere with each other constructively and destructively, forming a sound beam shaped like an hourglass.

Divergence

The extent to which a sound wave spreads out or diverges as it propagates into the far field.

Divergence Example

Small diameter crystals produce more divergent beams, like a pen light (can only see a short distance before it diverges).

Larger diameter crystals produce less divergent beams, like a search light (can see much farther before it diverges).

Lateral Resolution (continued)

Angular Resolution equals approximately the distance of the sound pulse.
Most imaging systems have better Axial Resolution than Lateral Resolution. Therefore Lateral Resolution will have a higher numerical value than Axial Resolution. This occurs because a sound pulse is typically wider than half of its length.
With all other variables being equal, pulses made with higher frequency sound are somewhat narrower than low frequency pulses. Narrow beams improve Transverse Resolution.
To lower the numerical value of lateral resolution of an ultrasound system, the diameter of the beam must be reduced.

Spatial Resolution - An ultrasound system’s overall ability to accurately create images of small structures in their correct anatomic position.

Temporal Resolution - The ability of the ultrasound system to accurately display motion over time.

Frame rate determines temporal resolution: a higher frame rate provides better temporal resolution.
Temporal Resolution is not affected by pulse duration or the length of the pulse.
Improved temporal resolution is achieved by increasing frame rate. Shallower imaging depth, a narrower sector, lower line density, and fewer focal zones can increase frame rate.
A wider sector, increased line density, greater imaging depth, and additional focal zones decrease frame rate and therefore worsen temporal resolution. Transmit frequency alone does not directly determine frame rate.

Lowest Temporal Resolution - Color Flow Imaging.

Best Temporal Resolution - M-Mode.

Worst Temporal Resolution - B-Scanning.

Frame Rate - The number of frames or images created each second.

UNITS: per second or Hertz or Frames per second.

RANGE: Typically; 10 to 60 images per second.

Lower PRP equals more pulses per second equals increased frame rate.
Frame Rate is determined by the US system and is not directly controlled by the sonographer.
More frames per second result in 1) Greater accuracy in locating moving structures, 2) Less time allocated to make each frame, 3) Decreased Line Density.

Line Density - The number of acoustic lines per degree of angle sector.

AKA: Acoustic pulses per Degree.

The more lines per sector degree that a system provides, the closer the scan lines are squeezed together, and the greater the anatomical detail displayed. Therefore, the HIGHEST line density will have the best detail.
Line Density = Pulses/Image ÷ Sector

Imaging Depth - The complete depth sound travels per pulse.

Pulses Per Scan Line - The number of trips required for a single image line or the number of foci.

Frames/second - The number of images per second or frame rate.

Pulse Repetition Frequency (PRF) - The number of pulses per second.

Relationship between PRF and Frame Rate

PRF = pulses/image line × image lines/frame × frame rate
= pulses per picture × pictures per second
Pulses/line × imaging depth × frame rate × lines/frame ≤ 77,000 cm

Example #1:

Is it possible to image with 200 lines/frame, 15 frames/second and a maximum imaging depth of 20 cm?

Yes, sound travels at exactly 154,000 cm/sec in soft tissue. Sound must travel to the reflector and return to the transducer; therefore, pulses can only travel to a depth of 77,000 cm and back in one second.

You must multiply:

lines/frame × frames/second × imaging depth
200 × 15 × 20
(200 × 15 × 20 = 60,000 cm)
An ultrasound system CAN perform under these circumstances because 60,000 cm is less than 77,000 cm.

Example #2:

Is it possible to image with 20 images per second, to a maximum depth of 20 cm, and create each image with 200 acoustic pulses?

No, sound travels at exactly 154,000 cm/sec in soft tissue. Sound must travel to the reflector and return to the transducer; therefore, pulses can only travel to a depth of 77,000 cm and back in one second.

You must multiply:

lines/frame × frame rate × imaging depth
200 × 20 × 20
(200 × 20 × 20 = 80,000 cm)
An ultrasound system CANNOT perform under these circumstances because 80,000 cm is greater than 77,000 cm.

XII. Doppler Principles

Doppler Effect – A change in frequency based on whether the source of the wave and the receiver of the wave are moving closer together or farther apart.

If they are moving closer together, higher frequency.

If they are moving farther apart, lower frequency.

Doppler Shift – Audible Doppler.

UNITS: Hertz

In clinical imaging, red blood cells are the primary reflectors that produce Doppler shifts. Blood cells move constantly through the circulatory system and make up nearly 45% of the volume of blood.
Increased transducer frequency = increased Doppler Shift = More likely for aliasing to occur.
Decreased transducer frequency = decreased Doppler Shift = Less likely for aliasing to occur.

Positive Doppler Shift – When RBC’s are approaching a transducer, a positive Doppler Shift results.

The emitted frequency from the transducer is less than the reflected frequency.

In other words, moving closer together.

UNITS: Hertz

Negative Doppler Shift – When blood cells are moving farther away from a transducer, the Doppler shift is negative.

The reflected frequency is less than the frequency originally transmitted from the transducer.

In other words, moving farther apart.

UNITS: Hertz

Maximum Doppler Shift – Occurs when blood cells travel directly toward or away from a transducer.

An angle of 180 degrees between the sound source and the direction of motion exists when the cells travel directly away from each other.

Absence of Doppler Shift – The Doppler Shift is related to the angle between the direction of blood flow and the direction that the acoustic wave propagates.

Normal incidence indicates that the direction of the blood flow is 90 degrees to the direction of propagation of the sound beam.

The cosine of 90 degrees is zero. Therefore, zero percent of the true velocity will be measured and the Doppler Shift will be absent.

Speed – Describes how fast an object is moving with no regard to the direction of travel.

Velocity – Specifies both the speed and the direction of travel.

Cosine – Cosine of 90° equals zero. Doppler shift depends upon the cosine of the angle between the sound beam and the direction of motion.

As the Doppler angle increases from 0° toward 90°, the cosine decreases and the Doppler shift decreases. At 90°, the Doppler shift is zero.
Imaging LOVES 90° (Normal Incidence)
Doppler LOVES 0°, HATES 90°.
Measured Velocity – Actual Velocity × Cosine.

What each term means

Reflector Speed = speed of the moving target (e.g., blood)
Incident Frequency = transmitted frequency
θ (theta) = Doppler angle between beam and direction of motion
Propagation Speed = speed of sound in tissue (≈ 1540 m/s)
2 = accounts for the sound traveling to the reflector and back
Included in the Doppler Equation is the COSINE of the angle between the direction of motion and the direction of the sound beam.

Incident Frequency – The frequency of sound emitted by a transducer.

UNITS: Hertz

Propagation Speed – The speed that sound travels in the body.

Continuous Wave Doppler

Aliasing does not occur with Continuous Wave Doppler.
CW can measure very high velocities.

Range – Depth.

Ambiguity – Not sure.

Range Ambiguity – Doppler shifts arise all along the length of the beam.

In other words, you don’t know exactly where the velocity is taken.

The spectrum produced by a continuous wave system displays Doppler shifts created by any blood cells intersecting the path of the sound beam.

Uni-Directional Doppler – Simply measures the presence of the Doppler shift.

Does not determine direction or velocity.

Bi-Directional Doppler – Distinguishes between positive and negative Doppler shifts.

In other words, it can distinguish whether blood is flowing toward or away from the transducer.

It does determine direction and velocity.

Pulsed Wave Doppler – A single crystal in the transducer alternates between sending and receiving ultrasound pulses.

Range Resolution – The ability to identify a specific location for Doppler processing.

Sample Volume / Gate – The location or position of the cursor identifying the region where Doppler shifts are being measured.

Time of Flight – Depth of Sample Volume × 2 / Propagation Speed.

Alias – Having a false identity.

Aliasing – An improper representation of a Doppler signal where very high velocities in one direction look like they are going backwards.

It occurs when the Doppler shift exceeds a particular value called the Nyquist Limit.

Continuous Wave Doppler cannot alias.
Aliasing is less likely to occur with a high PRF.

Aliasing occurs when the Doppler frequency exceeds half the PRF.

If the PRF is high, it is less likely that the Doppler shift will reach a value that is greater than half of the PRF.

Aliasing Reduction – The actions that will reduce the likelihood of aliasing are:

Increasing the PRF by selecting a shallower imaging depth.
Using a transducer with a lower frequency.
Using a continuous wave Doppler system that cannot alias.
If the sonographer selects an imaging view with a greater pulse repetition period, the PRF will decrease, and the likelihood of aliasing will increase.

Nyquist Limit – The Doppler frequency at which aliasing occurs.

Nyquist Limit = Pulse Repetition Frequency ÷ 2
The highest Doppler Shift that can be displayed without aliasing is the Nyquist Limit.

Duplex – Machines that display images and Doppler simultaneously.

In other words, the scanner displays both Doppler and two-dimensional data.

XIII. Ultrasound System Instrumentation

Master Synchronizer – Coordinates all of the components of an ultrasound system.

Pulser – Controls the electrical signals sent to the transducer for sound wave generation.

The pulser determines the following: PRF, pulse amplitude, PRP, the firing pattern for phased array systems, and the frequency for CW systems.
Phased array systems have more complex pulsers than mechanical transducer pulsers.
The signal typically produced by the pulser of an ultrasound system used to excite the PZT of a transmitter may be in the range of 100 Volts.
The output gain of an ultrasound pulser determines the intensity of an acoustic pulse.

Greater pulser voltage produces a pulse with greater amplitude, intensity, and output power. Voltage does not determine the transmitted frequency.

PULSER MODES

Continuous Wave – A constant electrical signal produced by the pulse, in the form of a sine wave, stimulates the PZT.
Electrical Frequency = Ultrasound Frequency
Pulsed Wave, Single Crystal – A short duration, electrical “spike” smashes into the PZT, which then vibrates at its resonant frequency.

One electrical spike creates one ultrasound pulse.

Pulsed Wave, Arrays – Short duration electrical “spikes” stimulate the many crystals that make up the array.

Multiple electrical spikes create array.

Receiver – Accepts small voltages produced by the transducer as it responds to reflected echoes.

Ultimately, the signals are processed and prepared for display on a CRT.

FIVE functions of the Receiver

Amplification
Compensation
Compression
Demodulation
Threshold (Reject, Suppression)
The sequence of receiver operation is in alphabetical order.

Display – The visual presentation of the processed data.

The display may be a CRT (television), a transparency, a spectral plot, or a number of other formats or devices.

The component of the ultrasound system with the LOWEST dynamic range.

Refraction – Change of direction, or deflection of a sound wave that occurs when a sound wave is transmitted with oblique incidence.

Refraction occurs during TRANSMISSION of a wave from one medium to another.
Refraction depends only on the medium and the angle of incidence. It is independent of the sound wave.
For refraction to occur, a sound wave must be 1) obliquely incident to the border between two media, and 2) the media on either side of the border must have different propagation speeds.

Refraction Example: A straw placed in water appears bent.

Snell’s Law – Defines the physics of refraction.

With refraction, the relationship between the angle of incidence and the angle of transmission is defined by Snell’s Law. When the propagation speed of the medium that sound is entering exceeds that of the medium sound is in, the angle of transmission is greater than the incident angle.

Range Equation – The mathematical relationship between the average speed of sound in soft tissue and the time of flight and distance that the ultrasound travels.

Time of Flight – The amount of time between the creation of a pulse (go) and the return of that pulse after it strikes a reflector back to the transducer.

Reflector – A reflecting surface in the body.

13 Microsecond Rule – When an ultrasound machine creates a pulse and the pulse hits a reflector and then returns to the transducer in 13 microseconds, the reflector is one centimeter deep and for every additional thirteen microseconds that pass, the reflector is one centimeter deeper.

Depth = Velocity of sound × Time of Flight ÷ 2
How fast the sound wave is traveling × How long it takes to Go and Return ÷ 2
You divide by two in the above equation because it’s the time the pulse travels into the body and the time the pulse returns to the transducer.
In soft tissue: Depth = 0.77 × Go-Return Time
Since the average speed of soft tissue is known and is 1.54 km/sec, then the velocity of sound divided by two (0.77) would be the speed of one way travel.

Echo Ranging – Estimating distance from the go-return time.

The greater the Go-Return Time, the deeper the reflector.

Acute Angle – An angle less than 90°.

Obtuse Angle – An angle greater than 90°.

Right Angle – An angle exactly 90°.

Incident Angle – The angle at which the sound beam strikes the boundary or interface.

Reflection Angle – The angle of reflection back towards the transducer.

Normal Incidence – When a sound wave strikes a boundary at exactly 90°.

Oblique Incidence – When a sound wave strikes a boundary at any angle other than 90°. It can be acute or obtuse.

When reflection occurs with oblique incidence, the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence.
If incidence is not 90°, perpendicular, orthogonal, right angle, or normal, it is oblique.

Transmission – Sound that is transmitted continues in a forward direction as it passes through a medium.

Incident Intensity – The intensity or energy of a wave at the incidence, or just prior to the interface of boundary between two media.

Reflected Intensity – Intensity of the sound beam that is reflected. After striking the boundary, the intensity changes its initial direction and reflects back towards the direction in which it came.

Incident Intensity – Transmitted Intensity = Reflected Intensity

Transmitted Intensity – Intensity of the sound beam that is transmitted.

Incident Intensity – Reflected Intensity = Transmitted Intensity

Intensity Reflection Coefficient (IRC) – The percentage of intensity that reflects back from its initial direction. UNITLESS

Percentages are reported without units.
Reflected Intensity / Incident Intensity = Intensity Reflection Coefficient

Intensity Transmission Coefficient (ITC) – The percentage of intensity that transmits, or passes through in a forward direction. UNITLESS

Percentages are reported without units.
The IRC and ITC when added together equal 1 or 100%.
Transmitted Intensity / Incident Intensity = Intensity Transmission Coefficient

Absorption – The conversion of sound energy into heat. The major source of attenuation in soft tissue. (Approximately 80%)

Bone is a great absorber; therefore it attenuates to a greater extent than soft tissue.

Reflection – The return of a portion of the sound beam back towards the transducer after it strikes a reflector in the body. A source of attenuation in soft tissue.

Specular Reflection – An organized, regular, well-defined reflection produced when a sound beam strikes a smooth surface such as a mirror.

A boundary acts as a smooth, specular reflector when its surface irregularities are small compared with the wavelength.

Scattering – A disorganized, chaotic and random reflection produced when a sound beam strikes a rough surface such as a pot.

Lung/Air is a great scatterer, therefore it attenuates to a greater extent than soft tissue.
A boundary acts as a rough reflector when its surface irregularities are large compared with the wavelength.

Rayleigh Scattering – Scattering in many directions from reflectors that are much smaller than the wavelength, such as red blood cells.

Blood produces weak Rayleigh scattering because red blood cells are much smaller than the wavelength used in diagnostic ultrasound.

Half Value Layer Thickness – The amount of thickness or the depth of tissue that reduces the intensity of a sound beam by half. (How far does the beam travel into the tissue where the beam’s intensity reaches a point where it is half of its original intensity?)

Half Value Layer Thickness = 3 / Attenuation Coefficient
Half Boundary Layer, Depth of Penetration and Penetration Depth all = 3 / A.C.

Impedance – Comparing the density and speed of two media will determine if a reflection will be produced and if a reflection is produced, the size of the reflection will be determined.

UNITS: Rayls

RANGE: 1.5 million to 2.5 million Rayls

SYMBOL: “Z”

The greater the differences in the speed and density of the two media, the greater the reflection.
The smaller the differences in the speed and density of the two media, the smaller the reflection.
If two media have the same acoustic impedance, there is no impedance mismatch and no reflection at the boundary. Each medium still has acoustic impedance.
Impedance = Density × Propagation Speed
= Density of the Medium × Speed of the Medium

Determination of Total Attenuation at a Specific Depth – 7 STEPS

You will be given the Initial Intensity (W/cm²) of an ultrasound wave and the Frequency (MHz) and the Path Length (cm).
Find Attenuation Coefficient (dB/cm).
Multiply Attenuation Coefficient (dB/cm) by the Path Length (cm).
This will give you the Total Attenuation (dB)

Note: cm’s cancel out.

Total Attenuation = Attenuation Coefficient × Path Length
Find the Intensity Ratio. (Use the decibel intensity-ratio chart)

Note: Remember that attenuation is a decrease in intensity; therefore, decibels must be negative.

Multiply the Initial Intensity by the Intensity Ratio.
This will give you the amount of intensity at the specific path length given.

Density – The concentration of mass per unit of volume.

Density = Mass / Volume
It can be compared to Compressibility and Elasticity when evaluating propagation speed.

Stiffness – A material’s ability to maintain its shape, even when pressure is applied to it.

Source – The ultrasound system/machine/transducer.

Medium – The part of the body that the sound wave is traveling through.

Amplitude – The maximum variation that can occur in an acoustic variable relating to the strength and magnitude of the sound wave. It is the difference between the AVERAGE value and the MAXIMUM value of an acoustic variable.

UNITS: Any unit of any acoustic variable. Decibels (dB).

Amplitude = Maximum – Average or Amplitude = Maximum – Minimum ÷ 2
The initial strength or amplitude of a sound wave is determined by the vibration of the piezoelectric crystal. The greater the vibration, the greater the amplitude.

Power – The total energy within the entire sound wave relating to the strength of the sound wave.

UNITS: Watts

Power = Amplitude²

Intensity – A specific concentration of energy within a sound beam relating to the strength of the sound wave. In other words, a concentration of power within the beam’s area.

UNITS: Watts/cm²

Intensity = Power / Area  Intensity ∝ Amplitude²

Compressibility – The ability of a medium to reduce its volume when pressure or force is applied to it. The opposite of stiffness.

Elasticity – The ability of a medium to reduce its volume when pressure or force is applied to it. The opposite of stiffness.

In Phase Waves – Two waves occurring at the same time in which the maximum and minimum amplitudes are in sync.

Out of Phase Waves – Two waves occurring at different times in which the maximum and minimum amplitudes are out of sync.

Interference – Two waves occurring at the same time and the same location that overlap creating one single new wave that results from the combination of the original two waves.

Constructive Interference – The amplitude of the new wave is greater (bigger) than the original two waves. In Phase waves interfere in this way.

Destructive Interference – The amplitude of the new wave is less than (smaller) the original two waves. Out of Phase waves interfere in this way.

XIV. Artifacts

Artifact - A display error or image feature that does not accurately represent the anatomy or its true location, size, shape, brightness, or number.

A structure or echo may appear even though it is not actually present.
A real structure or echo may be missing from the image.
A structure may be displayed with an incorrect size, shape, brightness, or number.
A structure or echo may be placed in the wrong location.

Basic Assumptions:

Artifacts are caused by the violations of the following assumptions:

Sound travels in a straight line.
Reflections are produced by structures that are located along the main axis of the sound beam.
The intensity of a reflection corresponds to a reflector’s scattering strength.
The imaging plane is extremely thin.
Sound travels directly to a reflector and back to the transducer.
Sound travels at exactly 1,540 m/s in soft tissue.
The attenuation of sound is uniform along the beam’s path.
When any of these assumptions are violated, an artifact may occur.

Causes:

Artifacts are caused by the following:

Violation of assumptions that are incorporated into the design of ultrasound systems.
Violation of assumption of viewer.
Equipment malfunction.
Poor ultrasound system design.
Improper use of ultrasound system.
Physics of ultrasound.
Operator error / Viewer error.
Artifacts can mislead interpretation. Always verify with multiple scanning planes.

Artifact Types and Recognition

Axial Resolution Artifact

Closely spaced objects appear as one reflector.

UNITS: mm, any unit of distance.

AKA: Longitudinal, Radial, Range, Depth

RANGE: 0.3 to 1.0 mm

Lateral Resolution Artifact

Closely spaced objects appear as one reflector.

UNITS: mm, any unit of distance.

AKA: Lateral, Transverse, Angular

RANGE: 2 to 10 mm

Approximately equal to beam diameter.
Improves with focusing.
Smaller values produce better pictures.

Acoustic Speckle

A form of image noise caused by interference of small wavelets scattered from many tiny reflectors.

Appears as grainy or textured areas that do not correspond to actual tissue structures.
Can be reduced by spatial compounding or persistence.

Slice Thickness (Section Thickness Artifact)

Occurs when the ultrasound beam has finite thickness and structures above or below the imaging plane are included in the image.

Appears as false echoes within an anechoic structure (e.g., cyst).
Reduced by using thinner imaging slices or focusing in the elevational plane.

Refraction

The bending of the sound beam as it passes through media with different propagation speeds.

Results in a lateral displacement of structures on the image.
Artifact causes duplication or distortion of true anatomy.
Most common at boundaries with oblique incidence and differing speeds.

Reverberation

Multiple reflections between two strong reflectors or between the transducer and a reflector.

Appears as equally spaced echoes that diminish with depth.
Commonly seen between the transducer and the pleura, diaphragm, or bladder wall.

Comet-Tail Artifact

A short train of closely spaced reverberation echoes that merge into a bright tapering line extending below a small, highly reflective interface.

Common causes include surgical clips, metallic foreign bodies, and cholesterol crystals. Comet tail is a form of reverberation.

Ring-Down Artifact

A continuous bright band or series of echoes produced by resonance of gas bubbles or fluid trapped between bubbles.

Ring down is related to gas resonance and is not simply another name for comet tail.

Mirror Image

An artifact in which structures are duplicated across a strong reflector such as the diaphragm or pleura.

The true reflector and its mirror image are displayed equidistant from the reflecting surface.
Caused by sound reflection and redirection.

Multipath

Sound takes different paths to and from the reflector, arriving at different times.

Results in incorrect depth placement of echoes.
The reflector may appear deeper or displaced from its true position.

Side Lobes

Weak off-axis sound beams generated by a single-element transducer.

May create false echoes adjacent to strong reflectors.
Reduced by apodization and subdicing.

Grating Lobes

Extra beams produced by array transducers due to regular spacing of elements.

Can cause echoes to appear in improper locations.
Reduced by subdicing and apodization.

Shadowing

A dark shadow distal to a structure that strongly attenuates or reflects sound.

Occurs with bone, calcifications, or air.
Results from high absorption, reflection, or refraction.

Shadowing by Attenuation

Decreased echo amplitude beneath highly attenuating structures.

Occurs with bone, stones, and calcified plaque.

Shadowing by Refraction

Edge shadowing resulting from refraction or bending of sound at curved interfaces.

Common at the edges of round structures such as cysts or vessels.

Enhancement

Increased brightness distal to a weakly attenuating structure.

Occurs with fluid-filled structures such as cysts, bladder, or gallbladder.
Results from less attenuation through the structure compared to surrounding tissue.

Enhancement by Attenuation

Increased echo amplitude due to reduced sound absorption along the beam path.

Enhancement by Focusing

Brightening of echoes in the focal zone where the beam intensity is greatest.

Range Artifact (Range Ambiguity)

Occurs when echoes are placed too close to the transducer because a pulse is emitted before all echoes from the previous pulse have returned.

Seen at high PRF settings.
Can be corrected by decreasing PRF or increasing depth.

Propagation Speed Errors

Occur when sound travels through a region at a speed other than the system’s assumed 1,540 m/s.

Results in improper depth placement, the structure appears too shallow or too deep.
If sound travels faster than 1,540 m/s, the echo returns sooner and the reflector is displayed too shallow.
If sound travels slower than 1,540 m/s, the echo returns later and the reflector is displayed too deep.
A localized speed error can also distort thickness or shape: a slower region may appear thicker, while a faster region may appear thinner.

Dosimetry - The study of ultrasound exposure and its potential biological effects.

Mechanistic Approach

Based on understanding the physical mechanisms (e.g., heating, cavitation).
Predicts biological effects by analyzing cause-and-effect relationships.

Empirical Approach

Based on observation and data from laboratory and epidemiological studies.

Thermal Effects

Sound energy is absorbed and converted into heat.
Tissue heating depends on beam intensity, exposure time, and absorption rate.
Fetal tissues and bone interfaces are most susceptible to heating.

Thermal Index (TI) - Indicates the likelihood of tissue heating.

Cavitation

Formation and behavior of gas bubbles in tissue exposed to ultrasound.

Stable Cavitation - Bubbles oscillate but do not burst. May enhance microstreaming.

Transient Cavitation - Bubbles expand and collapse violently, producing localized shock waves.

Transient cavitation poses the greatest risk of bioeffect damage.

Mechanical Index (MI) - Indicates likelihood of cavitation.

Physical Vibration

Micro-vibrations may occur in tissue due to radiation force.

Thermal Mechanism

Describes bioeffects from tissue heating.

Cavitation Mechanism

Epidemiology

Statistical study of populations to evaluate ultrasound safety.

In Vivo

In Vitro

This chapter adds concepts emphasized in the current ARDMS SPI content outline while preserving the foundational physics presented throughout this reference.

Patient Care, Communication, and Ergonomics

Physics is applied within a complete clinical examination. The sonographer must verify patient identity, review the order and relevant medical or surgical history, explain the examination, protect privacy, maintain safety, and document images and preliminary observations according to institutional policy.

Image quality never replaces patient safety, privacy, communication, or appropriate examination protocol.

Sonographic ergonomics

Position the patient close to the sonographer whenever possible.
Keep the scanning arm near the body and avoid prolonged shoulder abduction.
Adjust the bed, chair, monitor, and system controls before scanning.
Use a neutral wrist position and reduce excessive transducer pressure when possible.
Reposition the patient rather than maintaining a sustained awkward reach.

Extended Field of View, 3D, and 4D Imaging

Extended field of view

Extended field of view, also called panoramic imaging, combines sequential image data acquired while the transducer is moved across a structure. The system tracks overlapping anatomy and constructs one image with a field of view larger than the transducer footprint.

Steady transducer motion, consistent contact, and minimal out-of-plane movement improve panoramic image registration.

3D imaging

Three-dimensional imaging acquires a volume of data rather than a single imaging plane. The volume can be reconstructed and displayed in multiple planes or as a rendered surface.

4D imaging

Four-dimensional imaging is real-time or near-real-time display of changing three-dimensional volume data. The fourth dimension is time.

Contrast Imaging

Ultrasound contrast agents contain gas-filled microbubbles that strongly interact with the acoustic field. Their nonlinear response can be separated from surrounding tissue signals by contrast-specific imaging techniques.

Low mechanical index imaging helps preserve microbubbles during observation.
Microbubble destruction increases as acoustic pressure and mechanical index increase.
Contrast imaging may demonstrate enhancement patterns and low-volume blood flow that are difficult to appreciate with conventional Doppler.
Contrast-specific imaging is based on the different acoustic behavior of microbubbles and tissue. It is not the same as simply increasing image contrast with processing controls.

Modern Transducer Concepts

Two-dimensional matrix array

A two-dimensional array contains elements arranged in both rows and columns. Electronic control in two dimensions permits steering and focusing through a volume rather than only within one imaging plane.

Three-dimensional and four-dimensional transducers

Volume data may be acquired mechanically, electronically with a matrix array, or by freehand scanning with position tracking. Electronic matrix arrays can acquire volume information rapidly without mechanically sweeping the transducer face.

Nonimaging transducers

Nonimaging transducers provide acoustic or Doppler information without constructing a two-dimensional grayscale image. Examples include continuous-wave Doppler probes and some therapeutic or monitoring transducers.

Transducer selection is based on examination purpose, required penetration, footprint, operating frequency, field of view, and whether imaging or nonimaging information is needed.

Preprocessing and Postprocessing

Preprocessing

Preprocessing changes data before it is stored in image memory. Because it changes the acquired data, the original information generally cannot be restored after the examination.

Transmit frequency
Output power
Overall gain and TGC during acquisition
Line density, sector width, depth, focal zones, and transmit focus

Postprocessing

Postprocessing changes the display of stored information after acquisition. It alters how existing data are presented but does not improve the original spatial or temporal resolution.

Read zoom
Gray maps and colorized B-mode maps
Some display brightness and contrast adjustments
Write zoom acquires more information from a smaller region and can improve displayed detail. Read zoom enlarges stored pixels and does not create new data.

Modern Image Optimization

Tissue harmonic imaging

Harmonics are generated as sound propagates nonlinearly through tissue. The system transmits at the fundamental frequency and receives at a higher harmonic frequency, commonly the second harmonic.

May reduce near-field clutter and some reverberation artifacts.
May improve contrast resolution and border definition.
Can reduce penetration because the received harmonic frequency is higher.

Spatial compounding

Spatial compounding combines images obtained from multiple steering angles. It can reduce speckle and some angle-dependent artifacts while improving border continuity.

Spatial compounding may reduce frame rate because multiple angled data sets are required to create the compounded image.

Frequency compounding

Frequency compounding combines image information obtained from different frequency components within the transducer bandwidth. It can reduce speckle and improve tissue differentiation.

Persistence

Persistence averages information from consecutive frames. Increased persistence smooths the image and reduces random noise, but excessive persistence can blur rapidly moving structures and reduce temporal resolution.

Edge enhancement

Edge enhancement increases brightness differences at boundaries to make interfaces appear more distinct. Excessive edge enhancement can create an artificial appearance and may exaggerate borders.

Color and Spectral Doppler Optimization

Color priority

Color priority determines whether weak color information or grayscale information is displayed when both occupy the same pixel. Increasing color priority allows more color to overwrite grayscale. Excessive priority can place color in inappropriate areas.

Color map

The color map assigns displayed colors to direction, velocity, variance, or power information. The color bar must be reviewed because color selection alone does not prove arterial or venous flow and does not independently indicate oxygenation.

Low-flow imaging

Lower the color or power Doppler scale or PRF.
Lower the wall filter.
Increase color gain until noise appears, then reduce it slightly.
Use an appropriate transmit frequency and a small region of interest.
Reduce transducer pressure when compression may eliminate slow flow.
Power Doppler is sensitive to the presence of low-volume flow but ordinarily does not display direction or precise velocity.

Tissue Doppler

Tissue Doppler is designed to display the relatively strong, low-frequency Doppler shifts produced by moving tissue. Conventional blood-flow Doppler usually suppresses these signals with the wall filter.

Infection Control and Equipment Integrity

Transducer cleaning and disinfection depend on the examination, contact type, manufacturer instructions, and institutional infection-control policy.

Inspect the lens, housing, strain relief, cable, and connector before use.
Remove a transducer from service when cracks, exposed wiring, delamination, intermittent dropout, overheating, or electrical concerns are identified.
Document failed integrity or safety checks and notify the appropriate department.
Acceptable image quality does not prove that a damaged transducer or ultrasound system is safe to use.

Gray-Scale Quality Assurance

A tissue-mimicking phantom provides stable targets that allow current system performance to be compared with baseline measurements.

Vertical distance accuracy evaluates measurements parallel to the sound beam.
Horizontal distance accuracy evaluates measurements perpendicular to the sound beam.
Axial resolution evaluates separation of targets along the beam path.
Lateral resolution evaluates separation of side-by-side targets.
Dead-zone testing evaluates the minimum depth at which reliable echoes can be displayed.
Maximum depth of visualization evaluates sensitivity and penetration.
Uniformity testing evaluates whether similar tissue is displayed consistently across the image.
Quality assurance is based on repeatable comparison with an established baseline. A sudden deviation requires prompt investigation.

Sensitivity and Specificity

Sensitivity = True Positives ÷ (True Positives + False Negatives)

Sensitivity describes how well a test identifies people who have the condition. A highly sensitive test has few false-negative results.

Specificity = True Negatives ÷ (True Negatives + False Positives)

Specificity describes how well a test identifies people who do not have the condition. A highly specific test has few false-positive results.

Sensitivity focuses on detecting disease. Specificity focuses on correctly excluding disease.

Current Low-Flow and Advanced Processing

Newer systems may use advanced clutter suppression, coherent processing, high-frame-rate acquisition, or proprietary microvascular modes to separate very slow blood flow from tissue motion. These methods extend the principles of color and power Doppler rather than replacing the underlying physics.

The registry-level foundation remains the same: flow detectability depends on Doppler shift, signal strength, ensemble processing, gain, scale, wall filter, frequency, angle, motion, and transducer pressure.
